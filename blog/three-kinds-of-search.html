Three Kinds of Search, and Why Your Site Only Handles One

July 9, 2026 Updated July 9, 2026 7 min read

TL;DR Your website is good at a game that is quietly being replaced. There is no longer one kind of search. There are three, and they reward different things. The first is ranking on Google, which your website was built for. The second is being quoted by an AI engine when someone asks it a direct question, which requires stating things plainly and backing them up. The third is being recommended in a generated short list, which is assembled from what the whole web seems to agree about you, not one page you control. Same website, three games. Most owners only play the oldest and quietly lose ground on the two that are growing. You are spending real money to be discovered. The only question worth asking is whether you are spending it on the game your customers actually play now.

Your website is good at a game that is quietly being replaced. I learned that sitting with a winery owner last month who had just paid for a beautiful new site. Fast, clean, ranks first page of Google for her region. By every metric she was handed, she had won, and the check was worth it.

Then we ran one more test. We opened an AI assistant and asked it to recommend tasting rooms in her area. It named four places. It never named hers once. Fifteen years of work, three of the recommendations a few miles from her door, and to the machine she did not exist. Her site was excellent at a game that is fading, and invisible at the two games that are taking over. That is not a her problem. It is happening to almost everyone, because almost every website on the coast was built for a world where search meant one thing, and it does not anymore.

Here is the part nobody explains: there is no longer just one kind of search. There are three, and each one rewards something different.

What are the three kinds of search?

There are now three distinct ways a customer can find you, and each one rewards something different. The first is the search you already know, where Google hands back ten blue links and you fight to rank near the top. The second is being quoted, where someone asks an engine a direct question and it answers in its own words, pulling from a source it trusts. The third is being recommended, where someone asks an engine to plan something and it returns a short list of named businesses. People in this corner of the world have given each one an ugly acronym, but the acronyms matter less than the simple fact that they are three separate contests with three separate rulebooks. Your website was almost certainly built to win exactly one of them. I walked through the underlying mechanics in more depth in SEO vs GEO vs AEO, explained, and this piece is the plain-language companion to it.

What is SEO and what is it good at?

SEO, search engine optimization, is the game of ranking among Google's ten links, and it is the one your website was built to play. For two decades this was the only contest that existed. You wrote pages with the right words, earned links from other sites, made the thing load fast, and Google rewarded you with a higher spot in the results. When you click a link and land on a page, that page got its shot at you because it won at SEO. There is nothing wrong with this game. It still matters, a lot of buying still starts with a Google search, and the winery owner above was genuinely good at it. The trap is assuming it is the whole field. SEO gets you into the list of links. It does nothing to make sure an AI engine quotes you or recommends you, because those engines are not handing back a list of links at all. They are doing something else entirely.

What is AEO, being quoted by an AI engine?

AEO, answer engine optimization, is the game of being the source an engine quotes when someone asks it a direct question. Ask an assistant "what is the difference between a wine club and a wine subscription" or "do tasting rooms near Paso Robles take walk-ins" and it does not return ten links. It answers in a sentence or two, in its own voice, and it builds that answer out of sources it decided were clear and trustworthy. To be one of those sources, your site has to state things plainly and back them up. A page that says "we offer an unparalleled, curated experience" gives an engine nothing to quote. A page that says "our tasting room is open Thursday through Sunday, walk-ins welcome, reservations required for groups over six" gives it something it can lift word for word. Most websites are written to sound nice, not to be quoted. That single habit is why so many good businesses never get pulled into an answer. The engine could not find a clear, checkable sentence to use, so it used someone else's.

What is GEO, being recommended in a generated answer?

GEO, generative engine optimization, is the game of being named when an engine builds a recommendation. Ask an assistant to plan a weekend in your region, or to suggest the best place to do a specific thing near a specific town, and it returns a short list of named businesses. This is the contest the winery owner lost. Here is the hard part: that short list is not assembled from one page you control. It is built from what the whole web seems to agree about you, your own site plus reviews, directories, press, mentions, and the general shape of your reputation as the engine has absorbed it. You cannot simply write a better homepage and win this one. You influence it by being consistently, clearly present across the places the engine reads, so that when it reaches for businesses to name, the evidence keeps pointing back to you. This is the newest game and the hardest to fake, which is exactly why it is worth getting right early, before your competitors notice the field even exists. I made a related point about teams optimizing for the wrong engine entirely in when your marketing team is on the wrong search engine.

Why does your website only handle one of the three?

Your website handles only the first game because that is the only game that existed when the people who build websites learned their craft. A web designer, a marketing agency, a template, a brief, all of it was shaped by twenty years of Google ranking being the entire definition of getting found. So the site you paid for is tuned for links. It is fast, it has the right keywords, it earns its rank. Nobody on that project was thinking about whether an engine could quote a clean sentence off your page, because that was not a thing you could buy or measure when the site was scoped. Same website, three games, and it was only ever coached for one. This is not negligence. It is timing. The other two games grew up faster than the website industry adjusted, and the result is a coast full of expensive, beautiful sites that win the contest that is shrinking and sit out the two that are growing.

What does this mean for you?

What this means is that being found is now a budget question, not a website question. You are spending real money to be found, on the site itself, on the agency retainer, on the ad spend that points back to the site. The honest question is whether that money is aimed at the game your customers actually play now. If a meaningful and growing share of them are asking an assistant for a recommendation instead of scrolling Google, then a budget aimed entirely at SEO is a budget aimed at where the customer used to stand. That is the lens I bring to this work as a finance partner, not a marketer. I am not here to sell you more search. I am here to ask whether the dollars you already spend are pointed at the right field. That is the same reason Main Street IQ treats AI visibility as a finance question: it is money out the door against a result, and a result you cannot see is a cost you cannot manage.

Your Monday-morning next step

Monday morning, run one query each way and watch where you land. First, do a plain Google search for what you sell in your town, the old game, and find your rank. Second, open any AI assistant and ask it a direct question a customer would ask about your kind of business, and see whether it quotes you or someone else. Third, ask that same assistant to recommend businesses like yours near your town, and see whether your name appears in the list at all. Twenty minutes, three results, and you will know in your gut which games you are actually winning. Most owners are surprised, and not in a good way, by games two and three.

When was the last time you checked whether a search engine could quote your business, rather than just rank it? Ranking was the whole job for twenty years. It is now one game of three, and the other two are the ones your customers are quietly moving toward.

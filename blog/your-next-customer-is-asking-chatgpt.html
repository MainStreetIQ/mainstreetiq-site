Your Next Customer Is Asking ChatGPT, Not Google

July 2, 2026 Updated July 2, 2026 6 min read

TL;DR Your next customer is not searching for wineries on Google. She is asking ChatGPT which ones to visit, and the engine hands back a short list of three to five names, not ten blue links. If your winery is not on that list, there is no page two to fall back on. You are simply not part of the conversation. Read this the way you would read a P&L: discoverability is a leading indicator of revenue. Before a single dollar enters your tasting room, an engine has already decided whether to mention you to the person holding the phone. Most owners have never once checked what these engines conclude about them. Nobody can honestly promise you the top of any list, but you can find out today exactly what they say, and that is the move.

A winery owner on the Central Coast did something simple a few weeks ago. She opened an AI assistant and asked it to plan a wine weekend in her own area. It came back fast and clean: five tasting rooms, a lunch spot, a scenic drive between them. A genuinely good itinerary, the kind she would have been proud to hand a guest. Her winery, located just a few miles from three of the places it recommended, was not on the list.

She had spent fifteen years building that brand. Real wine, real reviews, a tasting room people drive out of their way to find. And to the AI that just planned a stranger's weekend in her own backyard, she did not exist. That is the moment this whole shift stops being abstract. It is a significant change that many owners still have not recognized, and it is happening quietly to businesses up and down the coast right now. For the past twenty years, getting discovered online meant ranking on Google: competing for one of ten blue links. The game has changed underneath everyone, and most owners have not looked up yet.

How is AI search different from Googling?

AI search is different because it returns a recommendation, not a list of places to look. When you Google "wineries near Paso Robles," you get a page of links and a map, and you do the sorting yourself. When you ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini "Where should I go wine tasting near Paso this weekend?" you do not get a long list of links. You get a short list of names, maybe three to five, with a reason for each. The engine has already read the web, weighed what it found, and done the sorting for you. You are no longer browsing options. You are being handed an answer.

That changes the math of visibility completely. On Google, being on page two was bad, but it was survivable. A determined buyer could scroll, refine the search, and dig past the first few results to find you. In an AI answer there is no page two to fall back on. There is the short list it gave, and everything it left off. The engine names three, maybe five, and then it stops. If your winery is not on that list, you simply are not part of the conversation, and the buyer never sees the gap, because they never asked for a tenth option.

What is a recommendation engine actually doing?

A recommendation engine is reading everything it can find about you and the businesses around you, then deciding who is worth naming. It pulls from your own site, from third-party listings and directories, from reviews, from articles and roundups, from whatever the broader web seems to agree on about your category in your area. Then it synthesizes all of that into a short, confident answer. It is not running a popularity contest exactly, and it is not following a single ranking formula you can game. It is forming an opinion about your business and acting on it.

The practical consequence is that the engine's picture of you is built from inputs you mostly do not control directly but absolutely influence. If your site is thin, your listings are stale, and nobody outside your own walls describes what makes you worth visiting, the engine has little to work with and tends to reach for the names that are easier to summarize. This is why the distinction between old SEO and the new layer matters so much, and it is worth understanding the difference between SEO, GEO, and AEO before you assume your existing marketing already covers it. It usually does not, because it was built for a different machine.

Why does this matter to your revenue, not just your marketing?

As a finance person, I find this particularly concerning, and not just as a marketing problem. To me, discoverability is a leading indicator of revenue, the same way pipeline is a leading indicator of bookings. On a P&L you learn to care about the things that move before the revenue line does, and whether an engine mentions you is one of those things. It happens upstream of every other metric you track. Before a single dollar enters your tasting room, before a reservation gets booked, before a club signup, an AI has already decided whether to put your name in front of the person holding the phone.

That is what makes the blind spot so expensive. You can have a strong month and never know that a steady stream of would-be customers got routed to three competitors by an assistant they trusted. The loss does not show up as a refund or a complaint. It shows up as demand that never materialized, attributed to nothing, because there is nothing to attribute it to. This is the same blind spot I keep running into when I tell owners their marketing team is optimizing for the wrong search engine. The effort is real. It is just pointed at a channel that is quietly handing share to one nobody is watching.

Can you guarantee a spot on the list?

No. I will not claim that you will always be at the top of any list, and nobody can honestly make that promise. These engines do not publish their ranking rules, they change constantly, and the answer can vary from one phrasing of a question to the next. Anyone promising you the top of the list is selling a guarantee they cannot keep, the same way the worst SEO vendors did fifteen years ago.

What you can do is far more useful and far more honest. You can quickly find out exactly what these engines say about your winery right now, which most owners have never once checked. You can see whether you appear at all, who appears instead of you, and what those competitors have given the engine to work with that you have not. And then you can improve the inputs: the clarity of your own site, the accuracy of your listings, the volume and quality of what the broader web says about you. You cannot control the verdict, but you can change the evidence the engine reads before it reaches one. That is the part within reach, and it is the part most businesses are leaving completely untouched.

What does this mean for you?

What this means is that "we rank well on Google" is no longer the same thing as "our next customer can find us," and the gap between those two is widening. If your entire visibility strategy was built for search results pages, you are defending a channel while a newer one quietly reroutes the customers who used to arrive through it. A strong Google position still matters, and it is also only part of the picture now. The other part is whether the engines doing the recommending have any reason to say your name when someone asks where to go this weekend.

This is the work I built Main Street IQ around, and it is why we look closely at AI visibility for the owners we partner with, including the wineries we work with up and down the California coast. The job is not to chase a guarantee nobody can offer. It is to treat discoverability like the leading indicator it is: measure it, watch it, and improve the inputs you can actually move, so that when your next customer asks an engine for a recommendation, you are in the running instead of invisible.

Your Monday-morning next step

Monday morning, open ChatGPT and ask it to recommend places like yours in your area, the way a real customer planning a visit would phrase it: "Where should I go wine tasting near me this weekend?" Read the answer carefully. Note whether your winery is mentioned at all, and if it is not, note exactly who showed up instead. That short list is the one your next customer may see before they ever see you, and the few minutes it takes to run the test will tell you more about your real position than another month of watching your Google rankings.

So before your next customer opens that app and asks the question for you, ask it yourself first. Is your winery mentioned in the answer?

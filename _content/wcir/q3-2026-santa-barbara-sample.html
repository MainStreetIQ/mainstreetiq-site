A Letter from Scott

I have spent a good portion of 2026 visiting tasting rooms, cellars and offices across Santa Barbara and San Luis Obispo counties. I have sat down with nearly 100 winemakers, owners, investors and people who work around this industry, and I’m now fortunate to count several of those wineries as clients.

Everyone I talk to wants to grow. Not everyone wants to grow the same way. One winery’s license will not let it pour by the glass, so it lives on bottles and club. Another does most of its business by the glass. Another opens on weekends to pour and talk, and $750,000 a year is exactly the business its owner wants. These are different businesses with different strategies, and the help on offer, from vendors and sometimes from the organizations that serve the region, too often arrives as one approach for all of them.

That matters more now, because the market is changing under every one of them. Grapes fetch less a ton, growers statewide are pulling vines, and more visitors seem to ask an AI assistant where to taste before they ask anyone else. I do not think that pressure lands evenly. Over the next year this report will look at which business models hold up and which feel the squeeze, starting with the direct-to-visitor side we measure today and widening to wholesale, which we have barely touched.

What follows is what we measured this quarter.

Scott Hess

The Market Around You

What’s Happening in Santa Barbara

Santa Barbara County’s wine grapes were worth $87.8 million in 2025, up 24% on 2024, according to the county crop report published this June [1]. The county is ocean-influenced and cool-climate, with Pinot Noir and Chardonnay across the Sta. Rita Hills, Santa Ynez Valley, and Santa Maria Valley, most of it within a short drive of the rest. That is the good news. The harder news is that a strong region does not automatically make any single winery easy to find, and many visitors now decide where to taste long before they arrive.

The grape market in Santa Barbara County

The crop report credits that 2025 value to higher yields on fewer harvested acres, together with strong market demand for high-quality grapes: 10,649 acres picked in 2025, down from 12,126 in 2024. Harvested acres are the vines actually picked that year. USDA’s 2025 grape acreage report counts something wider, the wine-grape acres growers report standing, including young vines not yet bearing, and puts the county at 14,854 acres, down only 0.9%, against 3.7% statewide on the same basis.

Prices are the hard part. USDA’s Final Grape Crush report, which records the average price paid per ton, puts District 8, the state’s grape pricing district for San Luis Obispo, Santa Barbara and Ventura counties, at $1,751.14 a ton for the 2025 crop, down 6.9% from $1,881.03 for the 2024 crop, against a 3.9% fall statewide. Reds took the cut, down 8.6%; whites held, up 0.3%. Allied Grape Growers, as reported by the Associated Press, estimates that about half of California’s 2026 crop went into harvest without a buyer contract, against 70 to 80% contracted in a typical year. We found no published count for the county.

Fruit is a buyer’s market this year. A winery that buys grapes has room to secure better fruit or longer terms, and an estate with surplus fruit has more reason to bottle it and sell it direct, which is the part of the business the rest of this report measures.

Sources for this part: USDA NASS, California Grape Crush Report, Final 2025 (April 30, 2026); USDA NASS, California Grape Acreage Report, 2025 Crop (April 29, 2026); Santa Barbara County Agricultural Commissioner, Crop & Livestock Report 2025 (June 23, 2026); Associated Press, "Farmers toil to sell grapes as wine demand drops" (September 27, 2026).

Santa Barbara County’s wineries are also paying for their own marketing. The county’s Wine Improvement District told the Board of Supervisors on September 1, 2026, in its annual report, that the “Let’s Go!” campaign for Southern California reached more than 16 million consumers and generated 138,000 website clicks, bringing about 66,000 new visitors to the website in two months [2]. Those are visitors to a website, not to tasting rooms. If your tasting room pays the assessment, it is worth asking the district how that traffic reaches the visitors who book with you.

The Central Coast Right Now

We think the COVID years, when tasting rooms were full and wine clubs swelled, were a gift that many in the industry mistook for a trend, and we do not expect those conditions back. Silicon Valley Bank’s 2026 State of the Wine Industry report puts the fall in national wine sales at 2% by volume in 2025, part of a decline that began in late 2017 [3]. Its 2026 Direct-to-Consumer report finds tasting room reservations about 2% below the year before in the twelve months to March 2026, and tasting fees, roughly double what they were in 2018, no longer climbing: Napa reserve tastings now average $125, and the share of wineries cutting their fees doubled last year, to 15.5% [4]. Those national figures set the price and the traffic every tasting room here competes on.

Younger consumers drink less, spread across more kinds of drinks, and show a lower baseline preference for wine [3]. Reading the same report, we think the wineries treating DTC as a relationship channel, not simply a point of sale, are the ones holding up.

That Napa figure is where the Central Coast has an opening. Tastings here cost a fraction of what a Napa reserve tasting now averages, and we think that accessibility is the region’s real edge. But it only helps if visitors can find you, and the way they find you has changed.

AI Is Changing How You Get Found

Some visitors now plan their wine country trips by asking an AI assistant instead of searching Google. They type questions like “I’m going to Santa Ynez this weekend, where should I taste?” or “best wineries near Solvang for a group”, and we think many follow the assistant’s shortlist.

This differs from search ranking. AI tools do not simply return a list of links; they synthesize reviews, press mentions, blog coverage, and web content into specific, named recommendations. A winery that ranks well on Google can be absent from AI results, and the wineries winning here are not always the same ones winning in traditional search.

Most wineries likely haven’t reckoned with this shift yet. A visitor who asks an AI assistant where to taste is likely to choose from what it names, and the assistant builds that answer from what it can read: your reviews, your listings, the lists and press that name you, and your own pages. That is why the score in this report is built around those signals, and why it comes second: it measures the signals our data ties to being named, and the section on who AI recommends measures the result.

Many visitors likely decide where to taste before they leave the house: checking Google reviews, scanning Instagram, reading TripAdvisor, and more and more often asking an AI assistant for recommendations.

Who’s Coming and Why They Choose You

Visitors come for the wine, the food, and the experience. Most are probably not studying AVA maps; they want a great afternoon with some education thrown in.

The closest recent survey of how visitors to this coast decide was done for San Luis Obispo County’s tourism bureau, Visit SLO CAL: an online survey by Heart + Mind Strategies of 3,000 travelers across California and key fly markets in July and August 2025 [5]. Nearly 4 in 5 of San Luis Obispo County’s visitors are repeat visitors and 69% booked within one month; for travelers who have not yet come, the decision drivers are past experience, word-of-mouth and digital content: articles, social media and influencers [5]. The same survey found that 67% of those travelers have visited Santa Barbara at some point, against 63% for Sonoma and Napa Valley [5]. Santa Barbara’s visitors, drawn from the same markets, probably decide in much the same way; the figures describe San Luis Obispo County’s visitors, not Santa Barbara’s.

A younger segment, solo travelers and Gen Z visitors drawn to destinations that blend adventure, wellness, and individual experiences, looks to be growing. In the SVB 2026 report [3], wineries investing in narrative and insider experiences report higher conversion, club acquisition and repeat visitation. We think the wineries offering seasonal events, food pairings, and behind-the-scenes access are the ones retaining and growing their club memberships.

How They Find You

Visitors reach a tasting room by three paths, and different signals matter on each.

The Pre-Planner. This person does most of their work before they leave home. They search Google for “best wineries in Santa Barbara” or “Los Olivos wine tasting weekend”, read travel blogs, and cross-reference TripAdvisor reviews. TripAdvisor is likely a starting point even when someone ultimately books elsewhere: they use it to compare, get a read on the vibe, and decide whether the experience fits a group or couple. Instagram probably validates the shortlist, and more of these visitors now plan with AI: MMGY Travel Intelligence’s summer 2026 Portrait of American Travelers, interviews with 4,371 U.S. adults in May 2026, found that 52% of active leisure travelers have used AI to help plan a trip, up 10 percentage points in a year, “with ChatGPT (57%) and Gemini (42%) emerging as the most widely used travel planning tools” [6]. These visitors often arrive with their stops already decided.

What matters: Content and credibility: travel blog coverage, strong review presence, and a feed that signals “this is what you’re walking into.” A winery not appearing on curated lists, or appearing with thin information, loses these visitors before the trip is booked.

The Hotel-Guided Visitor. The overnighter who arrives without a complete plan and relies on hotel concierge or tour operators to fill the itinerary. We think this is a significant channel in the valley, and likely the one most correlated with higher-spend profiles. Local drivers, concierges, and private tour operators can surface wineries visitors would not have found on their own. These gatekeepers build itineraries from personal relationships and firsthand experience, so we think they make the winery choice for some of these visitors.

What matters: Relationships. If the hotel concierges and tour operators in your area don’t have a reason to mention you, you are unlikely to be on this visitor’s itinerary.

The Spontaneous Visitor. Arrives in town, pulls out their phone, types “wine tasting Los Olivos” into Google Maps. This is where your Google Business Profile and your reviews do most of the work. These are often day-trippers or people who had wine country on the agenda but didn’t plan the specifics. In our own data, the number of Google reviews a winery has is the measure most closely tied to being found and named; the rating barely separates wineries once it clears a floor.

What matters: Google Maps presence and review volume. A visitor who has picked the region likely searches within it, so a winery missing from the map results for its own corridor misses the searches this visitor makes.

Once They’re In-Region

We think the wine trails and route maps the county’s wine and tourism groups publish work as default itineraries for visitors who want structure without hiring a guide. Being on a trail doesn’t guarantee traffic, but we think not being well-represented on one is a quiet loss. Word of mouth compounds in-trip: a visitor who has a great experience asks the tasting room staff where else to go, and in our experience those referrals bypass every digital channel.

How to read this call: the 95 is counted only among the 159 wineries whose own site we could read and check for a fee this quarter. The other 10 could not be read and count neither way. If we can read more sites next quarter, the count can rise for that reason alone, and we will say so when we score the call.

How We Measure

How we measure AI visibility

We put 75 trip-planning prompts about Santa Barbara County to six AI surfaces with web search on, as a visitor sees them (ChatGPT (search enabled), Claude (web search), Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, Microsoft Copilot and Gemini (current Flash, Google Search grounding)), three times over the month, for 1,324 answers in all. The prompts include several wordings of each question, because a visitor never types the question the same way twice. The result is an outcome, reported here on its own; it is not an input to anyone’s score.

Google AI Overviews gave 199 answers, not 225: on 20 of the searches Google showed no AI Overview at all, which we record as a measured absence, and 6 more showed one we could not read in full, so those count neither way.

We ask six AI assistants rather than one, and that is deliberate. They do not return the same answer. A winery that comes up readily in one can be entirely absent from another, so any single assistant would give you a flattering or a punishing read depending on which one we happened to pick.

These answers are not fixed. Ask an AI assistant the same question twice in the same week and the list of wineries it names will shift. A winery at the edge of visibility moves in and out of the results without anything about the winery changing at all. So treat a single appearance as encouraging rather than settled: the goal is not to appear in one answer, it is to be the winery an AI assistant cannot construct a sensible answer without.

How we compare quarters. The comparisons in What Moved use the panel we kept unchanged from Q2 2026: the same 12 questions per county, put to the same four assistants. This quarter we asked them three times; Q2 2026 asked once, so we show each run and call a change real only when it is at least as large as the spread between our own runs. We also re-read Q2 2026’s stored answers with this quarter’s matcher, the routine that finds winery names in an answer, so both quarters are counted the same way. That is why a Q2 2026 count here can differ from the one that edition printed.

On that panel, 95 of the 155 wineries in both editions were named in Q2 2026, and this quarter our three rounds of asking named between 94 and 98 of the 155. These counts run lower than the 153 of 169 in The One Thing That Mattered This Quarter for two reasons. The panel is smaller: 12 questions to four assistants, against 75 prompts to six on the full panel. And the counting rule differs: The One Thing That Mattered This Quarter counts a winery named in any of the three rounds, while these figures count each run on its own. Counted over all 169 wineries we measure, one run of those 12 questions named 97 to 100, and the three runs together named 113.

If you hold last quarter’s edition: it said 100 of 171 wineries were named by at least one AI assistant. That figure counted every winery in last quarter’s census, including some that have since closed or left it, named in answer to any question about either county. The comparisons in this edition use only the wineries in both censuses, on their own county’s questions.

The Rubric and How to Use It

“You should improve your digital presence” is not advice anyone can act on. This rubric shows where the points are. Its seven categories are built around the signals our own October test tied to being named by AI assistants and found by visitors. We deliberately did not score winemaking quality, critical acclaim, vineyard acreage, or tasting room aesthetics, because those are not what a first-time visitor can see before choosing. Every category measures something a winery can work on, though some, like the lists and press that name you, move slowly.

Every data point comes from publicly accessible sources, the same places your visitors look. No surveys. No self-reporting. No access to your private analytics. We read reviews, map and review listings, regional directories, search and map results, the best-of lists and travel press that rank for the searches visitors type, Instagram, and your own website. Separately, we put 75 trip-planning prompts to six AI assistants and track which wineries they name. That AI result is reported on its own and is not part of the score.

Two notes on method, because a number you cannot interrogate is not worth much. First, this edition is scored under rubric version v3.1, and every winery is measured at one location: several brands run more than one tasting room, and a score describes the room we measured, not every room. Second, this edition and the Q2 2026 edition were scored under different rubrics, so their points are not on one scale and we never subtract one from the other. What we compare across editions is position among the wineries in both; see the note under the rankings.

Your score reflects what a visitor actually encounters when searching for, evaluating, or trying to buy from you online. A high score means visitors can find you, your reviews inspire confidence, your site gives a visitor who is ready to book what they need, and your wine is easy to buy. A low score marks gaps we measured, and we think they are costing visits and DTC revenue over time; where a winery is marked * or § in the rankings, part of its low score is a gap in what we could read, not a finding. The encouraging part: 31% of the points in this rubric are things you can fix yourself, most of them in a few focused days. The rest take sustained strategy and patience, but they compound.

The Seven Categories

This report scores one thing: how findable and how convincing you are online. Its rubric has seven dimensions, and they are the seven below. If you have seen our website audit or our full business diagnostic, those are different instruments measuring different parts of the business, and they carry their own counts. Do not try to reconcile them with these.

Third-Party Presence (23 pts), do the lists, guides, and press that visitors and AI assistants read name you? Review Volume & Reputation (25 pts), how many people vouch for you, and are they still doing it? Search & Map Discovery (21 pts), when a visitor searches for wineries rather than for you by name, do you come up, in the results and on the map? AI-Ready Booking & Buying (14 pts), once someone has picked you, can they book, join, and buy without picking up the phone? Profile Accuracy (6 pts), does your Google profile state the right facts, and does Apple Maps agree? Citation Readiness (6 pts), when a search engine or AI assistant reads your site, can it get the facts out? Social (5 pts), does your Instagram confirm you’re open, active, and worth visiting?

The seven categories are not a flat checklist. They fall into two halves, and we report each half on its own, because a winery can win one and still lose the other.

Discoverability (“where should I go?”): Third-Party Presence, Review Volume & Reputation, and Search & Map Discovery. Whether a visitor, or an AI assistant answering one, finds you before they have your name. Most of the points sit here, because this is where our own test found the signals that go with being named, and most of the work here happens off your own website.

Conversion (“book it, reserve, show up”): AI-Ready Booking & Buying, Profile Accuracy, Citation Readiness, and Social. Whether the visitor who found you can confirm the facts, book, join, and buy. Most of these are one-time fixes.

A winery strong on Conversion and weak on Discoverability gets one clear instruction: the gap is off your own site.

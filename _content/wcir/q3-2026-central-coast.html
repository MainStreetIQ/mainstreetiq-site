A Letter from Scott

I have spent a good portion of 2026 visiting tasting rooms, cellars and offices across Santa Barbara and San Luis Obispo counties. I have sat down with nearly 100 winemakers, owners, investors and people who work around this industry, and I’m now fortunate to count several of those wineries as clients.

Everyone I talk to wants to grow. Not everyone wants to grow the same way. One winery’s license will not let it pour by the glass, so it lives on bottles and club. Another does most of its business by the glass. Another opens on weekends to pour and talk, and $750,000 a year is exactly the business its owner wants. These are different businesses with different strategies, and the help on offer, from vendors and sometimes from the organizations that serve the region, too often arrives as one approach for all of them.

That matters more now, because the market is changing under every one of them. Grapes fetch less a ton, growers statewide are pulling vines, and more visitors seem to ask an AI assistant where to taste before they ask anyone else. I do not think that pressure lands evenly. Over the next year this report will look at which business models hold up and which feel the squeeze, starting with the direct-to-visitor side we measure today and widening to wholesale, which we have barely touched.

What follows is what we measured this quarter.

Scott Hess

Who AI Puts on the Shortlist

45 of 442 wineries account for 47.5% of every time a winery is named by AI

This month AI assistants gave us 2,644 answers on where to taste in each county, and named a winery 23,432 times. The 45 wineries named most often, the top tenth of the 442 we measure, account for 47.5% of those namings; the ten named most often account for 17.4%. 54 of the 442 were never named in an answer to their own county’s questions.

And travelers are asking: MMGY Travel Intelligence’s summer 2026 survey of 4,371 U.S. adults found that 52% of active leisure travelers have used AI to help plan a trip, up 10 points in a year.

Our read: the shortlist is increasingly written by AI, and a small group of wineries is on it most of the time.

What Changed This Quarter

Since Q2 2026, 40 Central Coast wineries were named by AI less often, and 15 more often

We asked the same 24 questions as last quarter, three times each. Of the 393 wineries we measured both times (we measure 442 this quarter; the difference is wineries that joined our census since then), 40 came up less often and 15 more often. In Santa Barbara County 22 came up less often and 8 more often; in San Luis Obispo County, 18 and 7. About as many wineries were named overall; what changed is which ones.

Most of the drop came from Perplexity and Gemini, the two of the four assistants in the set we kept unchanged from Q2 2026 that search the web for every answer: the times these wineries were named went from 343 to 225, and of those 118 fewer namings, 99 were theirs. That points to a change in what those searches now find, not necessarily at the wineries. A fall here is a change in the answers, measured; it is not a finding about the winery. If yours is named, ask an AI assistant where to taste in your county and see who now appears in your place.

The Five Most Common Gaps

The five gaps we found most often across the 442 wineries in both counties, in the categories that take sustained work, each counted only where we could check. Under each, why it costs a winery.

1. Not in the search results for “wine tasting” searches: 338 of 442 wineries

A visitor who searches for wineries, not for you by name, never sees you in those results.

2. No press on the first page of its own name search: 332 of 442 wineries

Independent coverage is what tells a visitor, and an AI assistant, that someone else vouches for you.

3. Not in the Google Maps results for discovery searches: 273 of 442 wineries

The visitor searching the map from the car picks from what the map shows.

4. No new reviews in 90 days: 100 of the 429 wineries we could check

A profile that has gone quiet likely reads as a place that has gone quiet.

5. Few or no Google reviews: 72 of 442 wineries

Wineries with more Google reviews are named more often; that doesn’t prove reviews cause it, but it’s the strongest link in our data.

The Market Around You

The good news: our two counties held their value

In a hard year for California grapes, the county crop reports show our two counties held up. The Santa Barbara County crop report values its 2025 wine grapes at $87.8 million, up 24%; San Luis Obispo County’s crop statistics put its crop at $196.1 million, flat against $195.0 million in 2024. The same county reports elsewhere fell: Monterey down 25%, Sonoma down 17%, Napa down 6%. USDA’s 2025 grape acreage report, in the acreage growers report standing in its county tables, young vines included, shows acreage here slipping more slowly than the state’s: 1.9% in San Luis Obispo County and 0.9% in Santa Barbara County, against 3.7% for the state’s reported total.

The hard part: grapes fetch less a ton

USDA’s Final Grape Crush report, which records the average price paid per ton, puts District 8, the state’s pricing district for San Luis Obispo, Santa Barbara and Ventura counties, at $1,751.14 a ton for the 2025 crop, down 6.9% from $1,881.03, a steeper fall than the state’s 3.9%. Allied Grape Growers, as reported by the Associated Press, estimates that about half of California’s 2026 crop went into harvest without a buyer contract, against 70 to 80% contracted in a typical year. USDA’s estimate of all California wine-grape acreage, a wider measure than the reported acreage above, fell 6.9% in 2025, to 540,000 acres. In Paso Robles, the Wine Country Alliance’s benchmark of its reporting wineries shows tasting room visitors down 7% in the twelve months through August 2026, the same fall as its California benchmark, and revenue per visitor down 2%, against a 3% rise for California.

The good news again: the direct sale

Fruit is a buyer’s market this year. A winery that buys grapes has room to secure better fruit or longer terms, and an estate with surplus fruit has more reason to bottle it and sell it direct. The direct sale is the part of the business each winery controls, and it is what the rest of this report measures. Regions are betting on it too: Santa Barbara County’s Wine Improvement District reported to the county’s supervisors that the “Let’s Go!” campaign reached more than 16 million consumers and brought about 66,000 new visitors to its website in two months. The county editions carry each county’s own grape market.

About Main Street IQ

Main Street IQ gives businesses the kind of rigorous outside analysis that used to be reserved for companies big enough to hire a consulting firm. For wineries, that means an honest read on where you stand against the cohort, which gaps are most likely to be costing you visits and club signups, and a clear order to fix them in.
